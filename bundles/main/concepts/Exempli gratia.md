# Overview

***Exempli gratia*** (abbreviated **e.g.**) is a Latin phrase meaning "for
example" or "for the sake of example." It is used in English to introduce one
or more non-exhaustive examples that illustrate a preceding statement.

# Latin Etymology

The phrase is built from two Latin words in a genitive + ablative construction:

- ***Exemplī*** — genitive singular of *exemplum* ("example, sample, pattern").
  *Exemplum* itself derives from *eximō* ("I take out, I remove"), from
  *ex-* ("out") + *emō* ("I take, I acquire"). An *exemplum* is literally
  something "taken out" from a larger set to serve as a representative sample.

- ***Grātiā*** — ablative singular of *grātia* ("grace, favor, gratitude, sake").
  *Grātia* derives from *grātus* ("pleasing, agreeable, thankful"), from the
  Proto-Indo-European root *\*gʷerH-* ("to praise, to welcome"). In this
  construction, the ablative conveys the sense of "for the sake of" or "by
  reason of."

The full phrase *exemplī grātiā* thus translates literally as "for the sake of
an example" — the genitive *exemplī* specifies what the sake is for, and the
ablative *grātiā* expresses purpose or cause.

## Grammatical Analysis

This is a standard Latin idiom: *grātiā* (or *causā*) in the ablative governs a
preceding genitive to express "for the sake of X." The construction places the
genitive before the ablative (*exemplī grātiā*), which is the conventional
Latin word order for this pattern.

## Plural Form

The plural is ***exemplōrum grātiā***, where *exemplōrum* is the genitive
plural ("of examples"). It refers to multiple examples (separated by commas)
and is now rare in use. When abbreviated, it follows the Latin convention of
doubling letters to indicate plurals — "ee.g." or "ee.gg." — paralleling
abbreviations like:

| Singular | Plural | Meaning |
|----------|--------|---------|
| p. (pagina) | pp. (paginae) | page(s) |
| ms. (manuscriptum) | mss. (manuscripta) | manuscript(s) |
| f. (folio) | ff. (folia) | folio(s) |
| e.g. (exemplī grātiā) | ee.g. (exemplōrum grātiā) | example(s) |

# Usage in English

In modern English, e.g. is used before a list of examples:

> "Citrus fruits (e.g., lemons, limes, oranges) are high in vitamin C."

Key usage rules across most style guides:
- Preceded by a comma or used inside parentheses
- The examples that follow are non-exhaustive (contrast with i.e., which is exhaustive or definitional)
- Best confined to footnotes, tables, and parenthetical constructions rather than running prose

# Distinction from i.e.

| | e.g. (*exemplī grātiā*) | i.e. (*id est*) |
|---|---|---|
| **Latin meaning** | "for the sake of an example" | "that is" |
| **Function** | Introduces a non-exhaustive example | Introduces a restatement or exhaustive list |
| **Test** | "for example" | "in other words" |
| **Scope** | Open — there are other examples not listed | Closed — the list is complete |

These two abbreviations are frequently confused in English writing. The
confusion arises partly because both are Latin abbreviations, both use two
letters with periods, and both set off clarifying parenthetical material. [1]

The distinction matters: "Trees (e.g., oaks, maples)" implies there are other
kinds of trees not mentioned; "Trees (i.e., oaks, maples)" implies trees
consist only of oaks and maples.
