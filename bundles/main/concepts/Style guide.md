# Overview

A **style guide** (or style manual) is a set of standards for the writing,
formatting, and design of documents within an organization or field. Style
guides govern choices that standard English grammar leaves open: punctuation
conventions, abbreviation formats, citation style, capitalization, hyphenation,
and terminology preferences.

# Function

Style guides serve three main purposes:

1. **Consistency** — ensure all publications from an organization share a
   uniform look and feel, reducing reader distraction
2. **Efficiency** — eliminate per-document decisions about formatting minutiae
   by providing a single reference
3. **Authority** — provide a defensible answer to editorial disputes ("Chicago
   says…")

## British / Commonwealth

| Guide | Publisher | Notes |
|-------|-----------|-------|
| *New Hart's Rules* / *Oxford Style Manual* | Oxford University Press | Authoritative for UK academic publishing |
| *Fowler's Dictionary of Modern English Usage* | Oxford University Press | Usage-focused rather than comprehensive style |
| *The Guardian and Observer Style Guide* | Guardian Media Group | UK journalism; drops periods in "eg" and "ie" |
| *The Economist Style Guide* | Economist Group | UK journalism; "eg," with comma, no periods |
| *Style Manual for Authors, Editors and Printers* | Australian Government | Commonwealth government standard |

## North American

| Guide | Publisher | Notes |
|-------|-----------|-------|
| *The Chicago Manual of Style* (17th ed.) | University of Chicago Press | Dominant in US book publishing and academia |
| *AP Stylebook* | Associated Press | *De facto* standard for US news journalism |
| *The New York Times Manual of Style* | NYT | House style for the paper of record |
| *Editing Canadian English* | Editors' Association of Canada | Canadian publishing standard |
| *A Canadian Writer's Reference* | Bedford/St. Martin's | Canadian academic writing |

# Style Guides and Latin Abbreviations

The treatment of Latin abbreviations like e.g. and i.e. is a revealing test
case for how style guides operate. Every major guide agrees on the *function*
of these abbreviations, but they disagree on *presentation* — periods or no
periods, comma or no comma following. These differences are not driven by
national tradition (contra Bryan A. Garner's claim of a British/American
split [7]) but by individual publishing organizations balancing clarity
against typographic economy, particularly in the space-constrained environment
of newsprint. [12][13][14][15]

The variation illustrates a broader truth about style guides: they are
conventions, not rules of grammar. No guide claims the other guides are
*wrong* — each simply specifies what is correct *for its own publications*.
